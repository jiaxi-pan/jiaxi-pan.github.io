Reading

What I am reading on AI, creators and platforms, with a note on each.

Creator culture in China

Wallis, C. (2025). Social media and ordinary life: Affect, ethics, and aspiration in contemporary China. NYU Press. https://doi.org/10.18574/nyu/9781479825080.001.0001

Wallis follows young creatives who move from rural China to Beijing and use social media to develop their personal aesthetics and express themselves. In my study of audience engagement with AI illustration, I see something similar when an illustration moves viewers to make their own work. My question is whether people share AI work less with friends on WeChat than with strangers on Xiaohongshu, and whether some do so to build an audience they can later earn from.

Chen, Y., & Morris, C. (2026). Xiaohongshu (RedNote) as digital infrastructure of global China: Spatializing Chineseness elsewhere. Platforms & Society, 3. https://doi.org/10.1177/29768624261454784

Chen and Morris read Xiaohongshu as infrastructure that ties together people, places and businesses for Chinese life outside China, from fieldwork in Düsseldorf and New York City. I study the same platform through its creators and audiences, and I see a similar triangle of creators, audiences and AI art: what people see shapes which AI styles and tools spread. My question is about the “niche spots” hashtag in their New York case. Once creators tag a place as niche, everyone sees it. Is that another kind of platform influence?

Craig, D., Lin, J., & Cunningham, S. (2021). Wanghong as social media entertainment in China. Palgrave Macmillan. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-65376-7

Craig, Lin and Cunningham describe wanghong as a social media ecology grown from China's own political, cultural and platform conditions, different from its Western counterpart. My illustration study reads Xiaohongshu within this ecology and asks how it shifts as AI arrives.

AI, creators and audiences

Hwang, A. H.-C., Wong, S., Chen, B., He, J., & Do, H. J. (2026). “Better ask for forgiveness than permission”: Practices and policies of AI disclosure in freelance work. In Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (pp. 1-16). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791920

Hwang and colleagues find an expectation gap: freelancers often reveal AI use only when asked, while clients would rather be told, and unclear client AI policies widen the gap. Creators face audiences the way freelancers face clients, and my disclosure study shows both patterns: posts made with AI named it in the title, caption or tag, while two of three posts that used AI only for mockups said nothing until a commenter asked. Social media differs in two ways. Platforms label AI content themselves, and a creator answers not to one client but to many people with different expectations.

Zhang, Q., & Negus, K. (2026). Introduction to artificial intelligence in the creator economy: Creativities, adaptation, agency, and trust. Global Media and China, 11(3), 291-302. https://doi.org/10.1177/20594364261466625

Zhang and Negus frame AI in the creator economy through four themes: creativities, adaptation, agency and trust. They find most creators neither fearful nor excited about AI but pragmatic, using it for specific tasks, for platform incentives or to save time, as designers do when they use it for mockups. My pushback: some people try AI image tools out of curiosity, not to make art, and I am not sure that should count as creative work. They also note that suspicion of AI now shapes how audiences judge work whether or not AI was used, as when a review called a TV series so half-baked it could have been made with AI. I would like to know how that suspicion plays out in comment sections.

Wang, C., & Kim, S.-H. (2026). What is the future of AI-generated art? An AI-assisted netnography of resistance on Chinese social media platforms. Futures, 180, Article 103815. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.futures.2026.103815

Wang and Kim trace why users on Chinese platforms resist AI-generated art, naming missing emotion, a homogenized “AI flavor” and copyright, and show general users accepting it more over time while creators moved toward compromise. My study looks at the full range of audience responses, including the ways people take part.

Park, J., Oh, C., & Kim, H. Y. (2024). AI vs. human-generated content and accounts on Instagram: User preferences, evaluations, and ethical considerations. Technology in Society, 79, Article 102705. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techsoc.2024.102705

Park, Oh and Kim find that people rate AI accounts and their posts about as favorably as those of human influencers, and often could not tell them apart. My study moves from their lab setting to live comment sections, and adds Xiaohongshu.

Influencers and platforms

Cunningham, S., & Craig, D. (2019a). Creator governance in social media entertainment. Social Media + Society, 5(4). https://doi.org/10.1177/2056305119883428

Cunningham and Craig argue that creators are left out as stakeholders in debates on platform governance, and look at their interests from the top down, through platform and state rules, and from the bottom up, through creators organizing together. I would like to extend this frame to AI, where platform rules on AI content meet the choices of creators and the reactions of audiences. The paper counts fan communities as part of this industry, but much of the viewing and commenting I study comes from casual users who are not fans. Seven years on, with AI now a big issue in social media entertainment, I would like to know whether creators' interests carry more weight.

Duffy, B. E., Miltner, K. M., & Wahlstedt, A. (2022). Policing “fake” femininity: Authenticity, accountability, and influencer antifandom. New Media & Society, 24(7), 1657-1676. https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448221099234

Duffy, Miltner and Wahlstedt show how anti-fans on a hateblog police the authenticity of women influencers, in ways that fail to advance progressive gender politics. I also find authenticity policing in my study of creators' AI-use disclosure, where audiences ask how far AI went once they learn it was used. My question is how creators balance a favorable impression with authenticity, and what audiences expect of them.

Poell, T., Duffy, B. E., Nieborg, D. B., Mutsvairo, B., Tse, T., Arriagada, A., de Kloet, J., & Sun, P. (2025). Global perspectives on platforms and cultural production. International Journal of Cultural Studies, 28(1), 3-20. https://doi.org/10.1177/13678779241292736

Poell, Duffy and colleagues argue for studying platforms and cultural production beyond Anglo-American reference points. That is where I would like my comparison of Xiaohongshu and Instagram to sit.

Meisner, C., & Ledbetter, A. M. (2022). Participatory branding on social media: The affordances of live streaming for creative labor. New Media & Society, 24(5), 1179-1195. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444820972392

Meisner and Ledbetter argue that livestream audiences create commercial value through their participation and help build streamers' personal branding. In my study of audience engagement with AI illustration, I also saw how audience engagement, through comments and follow-up creations, shapes the conversation around a post and can potentially influence the creators. I wonder what it would be like to consider this more from a cultural perspective than a business one.

Cunningham, S., & Craig, D. (2019b). Social media entertainment: The new intersection of Hollywood and Silicon Valley. NYU Press. https://nyupress.org/9781479846894/social-media-entertainment/

Cunningham and Craig describe social media entertainment as a new creative industry, to which traditional entertainment has had to cede significant power. For my comparison, it is the Western counterpart to wanghong.

Beichert, M., Bayerl, A., Goldenberg, J., & Lanz, A. (2024). Revenue generation through influencer marketing. Journal of Marketing, 88(4), 40-63. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429231217471

Beichert and colleagues find that influencers with fewer followers bring a far higher return on investment than those with many, because their followers engage with them more. It shows why the engagement I measure in comment sections matters commercially.

Marwick, A. E. (2015). Instafame: Luxury selfies in the attention economy. Public Culture, 27(1), 137-160. https://doi.org/10.1215/08992363-2798379

Marwick examines the “Instafamous,” a new kind of Instagram celebrity, and how it relates to celebrity and tabloid culture. It helps me read Instagram as a place for display, which fits the admiring comments I found there.

Media and the self

Humphreys, L. (2018). The qualified self: Social media and the accounting of everyday life. MIT Press. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262037853.001.0001

Humphreys argues that people use media to document and share their lives, then revisit those traces to understand themselves. In my working paper, 32 of the 37 Chinese international students I surveyed reported using AI for self-reflection, which suggests that AI has become a new medium for what Humphreys calls media accounting. In my AI illustration study, I also saw audiences add their own follow-up creations in the comments. Digital media give more people ways to contribute, but that raises the question of whose contributions actually get seen.
