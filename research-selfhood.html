Working paper, November 2026

Negotiating Selfhood with AI: Chinese International Students' Experiences of Cultural Values, Language, and Self-Reflection

Authors
Jiaxi Pan, Boston University, and Seth Looper, founder of Lo/Be Lab
Data
43 survey responses and 10 semi-structured interviews
Method
Bilingual survey and semi-structured interviews, reflexive thematic analysis
Keywords
international students; generative AI; self-reflection; cultural self-construal; relational credit
Read the working paperOpen the PDF (26 pages)

Abstract

Chinese international students in U.S. higher education increasingly use conversational AI, including Western tools such as ChatGPT and Chinese tools such as Doubao, to reflect on their goals and sense of self. Drawing on the looking-glass self, self-construal theory and the Conservation of Resources model, this exploratory study combines a survey of 43 international students, 37 of them Chinese, with ten semi-structured interviews. Thirty-two of the 37 Chinese respondents reported using AI this way, most often for career and academic decisions. Students valued AI as a confidant that asks nothing back, which we describe with the tentative concept of relational credit. Some perceived AI as putting the individual first or as overly affirming, though these perceptions varied across tools. Students kept authority over the result, choosing languages and tools for different problems and rewriting or rejecting what AI offered. AI-mediated reflection appears less as cultural absorption than as a negotiated practice.

My part

  • I am a research assistant at Lo/Be Lab. Seth Looper supervises the study and brings the framing and the analytic method.
  • Helped revise the survey and interview questions.
  • Recruited participants through student networks: 43 survey responses and 10 interviews.
  • Ran the interviews, eight in Mandarin and two in English, and transcribed them.
  • Wrote the first analysis of the survey and the interviews.

Ethics and consent

Participation was voluntary, and every participant gave consent before taking part. The survey did not ask for names. Interviews were recorded with permission. Participants are identified by number, and details that could identify them have been removed.
